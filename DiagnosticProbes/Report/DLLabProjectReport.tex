\begin{abstract}
\vspace{0.5em}
  \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Orbis, V-JEPA, DoTA, Retrospective Surprise, Tubelet Size, Diagnostic Probes \newline
  Orbis proved to yield state-of-the-art performance in long-horizon prediction, especially in challenging scenarios like turning maneuvers and traffic. In this study, we perform an evaluation on Orbis and V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B for Out-of-Distribution (OOD) scenarios involving several anomalous classes of collisions. We show that Orbis, when trained upon mid-block activations can predict anomalies consistently better than the V-JEPA variant, for anomalies involving cars. While the V-JEPA model of 80M parameters also performs fairly well for obstacle and pedestrian anomalies, they often do not attend to the intended anomalies. Our study also concludes that the training-free retrospective surprise scores is a reliable metric for detection of anomalies, especially for classes like obstacles and pedestrians. Project codebase along with the links for poster, checkpoints and results can be found here - \href{https://github.com/praffulr/orbis/}{https://github.com/praffulr/orbis/}
  
\end{abstract}

\subsubsection{Overall Architecture}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:orbisArch}, the Orbis framework consists of two primary components: a Hybrid Image Tokenizer and a Spatiotemporal Diffusion Transformer (STDiT) world model. The Hybrid Image Tokenizer processes input frames through a dual-encoder design, comprising a semantic encoder guided by a frozen DINOv2 model and a parallel detail encoder. These paths yield continuous tokens ($x_s, x_d$) that undergo vector quantization to produce quantized representations ($q_s, q_d$), which are ultimately reconstructed back into the image space via an image decoder. 

For temporal modeling, the world model receives a sequence of encoded context frames $\mathbf{X}_{0:N-1}$ alongside a target frame. These representations are passed through a frame embedding layer and combined with conditioning variables via Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN). The core of the generative architecture relies on a Spatiotemporal Diffusion/Flow Matching paradigm, executing $M$ iterations of sampling denoising or unmasking to predict future frames $\mathbf{x}_N^0$.

\subsubsection{STDiT Specifications}
The Orbis World Model utilizes a Spatiotemporal Diffusion Transformer (STDiT) block structure acting as an autoregressive flow-matching backbone. In contrast to frozen feature extractors, this component serves a generative model objective for predictive video modeling. The complete model contains approximately 469 million parameters distributed across 24 Transformer blocks.

Each STDiT block is sequentially divided into consecutive attention mechanisms to handle the distinct dimensions of video data. Specifically, the tokens pass through a Spatial Attention block, followed by a Temporal Attention block, and are finally processed by a standard Feed Forward network. 

The architecture operates on video inputs with a resolution of $512 \times 288$ pixels. Driven by a hidden embedding dimension of 768, each individual frame is represented by a $32 \times 18$ spatial token grid, yielding a total allocation of 576 spatial tokens per frame:
\begin{equation}
32 \times 18 = 576
\end{equation}

\subsection{V-JEPA 2.1 Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:vjepa_architecture} illustrates the overall architecture of the V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B/16 model used in our experiments. The model receives a sequence of five consecutive video frames, which are spatially resized to $384\times384$ pixels. The input is processed using spatiotemporal tubelet embedding with a temporal tubelet size of five and a spatial patch size of $16\times16$ pixels.

The resulting tokens are processed by the Vision Transformer encoder to produce a spatial representation consisting of 576 tokens, each with an embedding dimension of 768. The pretrained encoder is kept frozen throughout our experiments, and the resulting representations are subsequently used as inputs to the downstream binary classification probes described in the following sections.

\subsubsection{V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B/16 Specifications}

The experiments use the pretrained \textbf{V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B/16} variant as the video representation encoder. The model has approximately \textbf{80 million parameters} and is used as a frozen feature extractor throughout the downstream experiments. No parameters of the pretrained encoder are updated using the DoTA anomaly labels.

The ViT-B/16 architecture uses a hidden embedding dimension of 768 and 12 Transformer layers with 12 attention heads. The model operates on video inputs with a spatial resolution of $384\times384$ pixels. With a spatial patch size of $16\times16$ pixels, each frame is represented by a $24\times24$ spatial token grid:

\begin{equation}
\frac{384}{16}\times\frac{384}{16}
=
24\times24
=
576
\end{equation}

spatial tokens. Each token is represented by a 768-dimensional embedding.


\subsection{Orbis Surprise Scores Evaluation}
We use Orbis' next-frame prediction loss to measure temporal surprise: low loss implies high confidence (expected), while high loss signals an anomaly (surprising).

\subsubsection{Per-Frame Surprise} 
For frame $k$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Encode past frame context up to step $k$.
    \item Encode the true next frame into latent tokens $z^*$.
    \item Sample a noise level $t \in (0, 1)$ and Gaussian noise $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ to build noisy state $x_t = t \cdot z^* + (1 - t) \cdot x_0$.
    \item Feed $x_t$, $t$, and context into the world model to predict velocity $\hat{v}$.
    \item Compute loss against true velocity $v = z^* - x_0$: $\mathcal{L}(t, x_0) = \|\hat{v} - (z^* - x_0)\|_2^2$.
    \item Repeat for 4 noise levels ($t \in \{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$) and 2 noise samples per level. Average the 12 loss scores to get the raw surprise score $S_k$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Sanity Check on Calibration}
To verify the stability of our scoring system, we split our data into 1,143 calibration samples, 357 held-out normal samples, and 1,500 out-of-distribution (OOD) anomaly samples. The calibration samples established our baseline distribution. As a sanity check, we calculated the Z-scores for the held-out and OOD sets against this calibration baseline (Table~\ref{tab:sanity_check}). 

The held-out normal scores closely matched the calibration baseline, yielding an overall mean Z-score near zero ($\mu_z = 0.0067$) and a standard deviation near one ($\sigma_z = 1.0568$). This successful alignment confirmed that our normalization parameters are highly stable, giving us the confidence to reliably scale up and perform our subsequent anomaly experiments on a much larger sample size. Meanwhile, the OOD population correctly manifested a higher overall mean surprise score and shifted Z-score ($\mu_z = 0.6480$).

\section{Evaluation Benchmark} 
The dataset consists of 4,677 video sequences with temporal, spatial, and categorical annotations, originally captured at a resolution of 1280×720 and a sampling frequency of 30 fps, though frames were extracted at 10 fps in the raw dataset and subsequently resampled to 5 fps to maintain consistency with Orbis.

For each of the sequences in the dataset, we sampled a context of 5 frames at 5 fps. This subsampling is done to stay consistent with the original experimental setup on Orbis, and to not introduce any additional variance in the studies. We derived Out-of-Distribution (OOD) and In-Distribution (ID) samples from each sequence, by leveraging the temporal annotations provided by the dataset.  When any sequence does not have enough frames before the beginning of the anomalous section, we also included frames from the end as an ID sample, for some of the sequences.

We used a sample size of 3000 samples and this sample was used to evaluate our classifiers for Orbis activations, Surprise attention and V-JEPA activations. We used a train-validation split of 80:20 and utilized 3000 processed samples from the processed DoTA dataset. The randomization process ensures that we do not explicitly sample OOD and ID samples from the same root sequences, while maintaining the 50:50 split for OOD:ID in the sampled 3000 clips.

From the available classes of DoTA, the curated dataset of 3000 processed samples has 9 classes in total - start-stop-or-stationary, moving-ahead-or-waiting, lateral, oncoming, turning, pedestrian, obstacle, leave-to-right, leave-to-left. We ignored sequences originally labeled unknown, and also skipped night frames by leveraging the annotations.

\section{Methods}

We experimented with linear probes with maxpooling and attention-based multi-head probes for all the of Orbis encoder embeddings, Orbis midblock activations and V-JEPA activations. In each of these variants, we found that attention pooling performs significantly better than the linear probe. 

For the probes trained on Orbis surprise scores, the attention probes were trained on normalized surprise scores for the same evaluation dataset, that was used for the other experiments.

While we also attempted to train a multi class classifier on the activations of Orbis, the inherent class imbalance of the dataset did not help achieve high performance metrics. We utilized the Class-Balanced Loss idea, proposed to handle the long tail class-imbalance problem by re-weighting the loss based on effective number of samples, in our multiclass experiments.

\subsection{Architectures of Diagnostic Probes}

For the attention probes, we used a lightweight attention probe to aggregate the 576 tokens into a single representation. A learnable query is applied through multi-head attention, with the V-JEPA tokens serving as keys and values. The resulting pooled representation is normalized and passed to a linear binary classifier.

Max pooling is used as a non-parametric baseline. For each of the 768 embedding dimensions, the maximum activation across the 576 tokens is selected, producing a single 768-dimensional representation for the binary classifier.

\subsection{Loss functions}

While the standard Cross-Entropy Loss works well for the balanced binary classification tasks, it was not very effective for the multi class experiments, due to the inherent class imbalance of the dataset. Despite experimentation with various variants of the Weighted-Cross Entropy Loss - aligning the class weights as an inverse of their frequency and by assigning a fixed weight of 0.5 for normal class and splitting the weights of anomalous classes as a function of their rarity - we were not able to achieve satisfactory results.

A decent performance of the binary classifier motivated the idea of using a joint loss function, along with a Class-Balanced loss function - which re-weights the loss based on effective number of samples.

The effective number of samples $E_n$ for a class $c$ with $N_c$ total samples is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    E_n = \frac{1 - \beta^{N_c}}{1 - \beta}
\end{equation}

The corresponding class-balanced weight $W_c$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    W_c = \frac{1 - \beta}{1 - \beta^{N_c}}
\end{equation}
where $\beta \in [0, 1)$ is a hyperparameter controlling the effective sample volume scaling.

\subsection{Choice for Mid-block Activations}

While the earlier features are tasked to learn about low-level visual features and representations, the higher level semantic features like anomaly detection classification are expected to be predicted better by the later activation Blocks.

Orbis has 24 STDiT blocks in the world model module, and by probing at multiple blocks, we observed best results from the activations of block 18. While, we similarly probed over the 12 V-JEPA blocks, we found significantly better performance on the prediction task from the final Predictor block. We believe this is due to the fact that V-JEPA is trained to predict robust embeddings which perform well across various downstream tasks, while the V-JEPA 2.1 works significantly well for the dense prediction tasks.


\subsection{Normalization Techniques}

For the surprise detection experiment, we chose 3000 in-distribution samples to get a calibrated mean and std-dev and used these to normalize our scores for Out-of-Distribution sample scores.

\subsubsection{Z-Score Normalization}
The surprise evaluation scores were normalized relative to the baseline distribution of in-distribution samples. Specifically, the empirical mean ($\mu_{\text{in}}$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{in}}$) were calculated from a reference set of $N = 3,000$ raw in-distribution clip scores. For any raw out-of-distribution (OOD) sample score $x$, the Z-score normalized value $z$ is computed as follows:

\begin{equation}
z = \frac{x - \mu_{\text{in}}}{\sigma_{\text{in}}}
\end{equation}

When visualizing these metrics as spatial overlays on images, the scores were bounded to a range corresponding to $1\sigma$ to $3\sigma$ deviations to highlight anomalous regions cleanly while suppressing baseline noise.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

\subsubsection{Min-Max Normalization}
A min-max normalization was also tried directly to the raw, unnormalized scores. To eliminate low-level noise and minor fluctuations, a hard thresholding operation was introduced at $\tau = 0.2$. Let $X$ represent the set of all raw scores, and $x \in X$ be an individual score. The normalized score $x_{\text{norm}}$ is defined by:

\begin{equation}
x_{\text{norm}} = \frac{x' - \min(X')}{\max(X') - \min(X')}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $X' = \{x \in X \mid x \ge \tau\}$ represents the thresholded score set, and $x'$ is the thresholded value of the sample.

\subsection{V-JEPA Experiment Setup}
We use the pretrained V-JEPA model as a frozen video feature extractor. No fine-tuning is performed using DoTA anomaly labels. Instead, V-JEPA representations are extracted once and cached for all subsequent probing experiments.
The temporal configuration is described separately below. In our experiments, 5 consecutive context frames are provided to the model and a temporal tubelet size of 5 is used. This allows the complete five-frame temporal context to contribute to the extracted representation.

We use a temporal tubelet size of five, and this configuration was chosen after observing that a tubelet size of two with five input frames left the final frame unrepresented because the temporal dimension was not evenly divisible by the tubelet size. Since the final frame contains the most recent information about the driving event, excluding it was undesirable for retrospective anomaly detection. Using a tubelet size of five ensures that the complete input sequence contributes to the representation. We also transform the image to a $384\times384$ size before passing it through the model, along with ImageNet normalization.

\subsection{Orbis Surprise Scores}

\subsubsection{Visualizing the Encoder Heads}
When we overlay the normalized surprise scores directly onto the video frames, we can clearly see how each encoder head behaves \ref{fig:zscore}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed Head:} The Detailed head almost always does a better job than the semantic head at catching the actual structure of the anomaly, although it can look a bit noisy around the edges.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Head:} The Semantic head is less precise and often misses the core layout of the anomalous event.
    \item \textbf{Combined Encoder:} The Combined head gives the cleanest results. It provides the best overlap with the actual anomaly, pinpointing the event while cutting out background noise.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Impact of Noise Levels}
Looking at the noise steps from $t = 0.2$ to $t = 0.8$ in \ref{fig:zscore}, there is a steady rise in the overall surprise factor. The heatmaps get brighter and much more focused as the noise increases. This suggests that higher noise levels actually help the model better understand and distinguish out-of-distribution events from normal backgrounds.

\subsubsection{Classifier Performance}
We also benchmarked how well these surprise scores perform when fed into downstream classifiers, comparing them against direct feature probing (Table 1):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surprise Scores:} The \textit{Combined} surprise scores yield the strongest results in this category with an accuracy of $0.69$ and an AUC of $0.75$. Looking at the individual heads, the \textit{Semantic} head yields better quantitative performance ($0.64$ accuracy) compared to the \textit{Detailed} head ($0.60$ accuracy), even though the Detailed head looks better visually.
    \item \textbf{Feature Probes:} If we look at training classifiers directly on the model's internal features rather than training-free surprise scores, probing the raw \textit{Orbis Activations} with an Attention Probe gives the highest overall results on the benchmark, reaching an accuracy of $0.77$ and an AUC of $0.85$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

We observed that the Orbis attention probe trained on midblock activations performs better for anomalies involving cars, than for classes like pedestrians and obstacles. By verifying that the midblock activations predict anomalies better than the naive encoder embeddings, we can attribute the performance to the capabilities of the world model rather than a direct consequence of the encoder architecture.

Training-free surprise score evaluation proves to be a good metric for anomaly detection and works best at high noise. We first verified that the mean surprise scores across anomalous samples are significantly higher than for normal samples, and that the surprise scores are similar for normal samples across different random sampled subsets of the dataset. This motivated us to use surprise scores as a heuristic for anomaly detection, and train diagnostic probes. We also trained separate probes for the Semantic and Detail heads in the latent space, and studied the performance at 4 different noise levels. 

The probes perform significantly for higher noise levels. Even though the probes trained on the combined encoder embedding perform the best, the semantic head performs better than the detailed head. However, the overlaid plots of raw surprise scores reveal that the Detailed head has better surprise on the anomalous regions. 

The V-JEPA variant achieves a decent performance on anomaly prediction, yet it often does not attend to anomalies.
We believe that the V-JEPA prediction is motivated by the proximity of objects in the scene rather than the actual anomalies.

While we limited this study to attention heatmaps of only the midblock activation probes, the attention heatmaps from the trained diagnostic probes from surprise scores would potentially reveal more information.

The DoTA dataset is curated from multiple Youtube videos where each sequence has varying lengths of anomalies, while all of them are modified to 10 fps. Each sequence of DoTA has corresponding temporal annotations, with timestamps of when the anomaly begins and ends. 

Each sample contains five context frames. This provides approximately one second of temporal context to the model while reducing the computational cost of processing the full video sequences.

The same preprocessing and sampling procedure is used for the V-JEPA feature extraction pipeline to maintain consistency across the training and validation samples.



\section{Hyperparameter Optimization}
For all the variants, Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimization (HPO) was performed using Weights \& Biases. The following hyperparameters were optimized:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Batch Size
    \item Learning Rate
    \item Weight Decay
    \item AdamW $\beta_1$
    \item AdamW $\beta_2$
    \item Early Stopping Patience
    \item Number of Attention Heads (Attention Pooling only)
    \item Dropout Probability
    \item $\beta_1$ (for Class-Balanced Loss)
\end{itemize}

All probes were trained using the AdamW optimizer together with Early Stopping based on the validation loss.
